\section{System Overview}

The implemented system is a complete \textbf{Flow Matching generative model} based on a temporally-conditioned U-Net architecture. The goal of the model is to learn a continuous-time generative process that maps samples from a simple Gaussian distribution to realistic images.

More formally, instead of directly predicting data or noise, the model learns a \textbf{velocity field} $v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_\tau, \tau)$ that describes how a sample evolves along a continuous trajectory from a base distribution (Gaussian noise) to the data distribution.

Given a source sample $\mathbf{z}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and a data sample $\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}$, intermediate states are defined through a probability path $\mathbf{z}_\tau$, and the network is trained to regress the corresponding instantaneous velocity that transports $\mathbf{z}_\tau$ along this path.

At inference time, new samples are generated by solving an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) defined by the learned velocity field, starting from pure Gaussian noise and progressively transforming it into structured images.

\subsection{The Main Pipeline}

\vspace{-0.4em}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{Pipeline_None.png}
\caption{\small High-level System pipeline designed by me during the project planning phase.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

\vspace{-0.6em}

The program \textbf{pipeline} is composed of \textbf{seven sequential parts}, starting from user-level configuration and ending with checkpointing and sample generation.

The \textbf{first stage} corresponds to the \textbf{"User Interaction and CLI Argument Parsing"}. In this phase, the \textbf{entire system is configured through command-line arguments}, which define not only the training mode but also all architectural and optimization hyperparameters. This includes dataset selection, image resolution, batch size, learning rate, optimizer configuration, and model-specific parameters such as channel multipliers and attention resolutions.\\
The purpose of this stage is to \textbf{decouple experimentation from code modification and to ensure portability across different execution environments}, including Kaggle Notebooks, Jupyter Notebooks, and Google Colab.
Once parsed, these arguments also define the run directory structure, ensuring that all outputs, including checkpoints and generated samples, are consistently organized. In addition, a deterministic seed is set at this stage, ensuring full reproducibility across CPU and GPU operations.

The \textbf{second stage} is the \textbf{"Dataset and Transformation Pipeline"}. Here, \textbf{raw image data is processed into a unified representation suitable for the U-Net training}. Images are converted into a consistent format through a sequence of transformations that include color space normalization (\textbf{RGB} or grayscale conversion), resizing to a fixed spatial resolution, center cropping to avoid geometric distortion, optional data augmentation through horizontal flipping during training, and final conversion into tensor format. After conversion, pixel values are normalized into the range \textbf{[-1, 1]}, which is essential for stabilizing the training dynamics of diffusion-style and flow-based generative models. This stage ensures that all datasets share a consistent statistical structure before being passed to the model.

The \textbf{third stage} defines the \textbf{"DataLoader construction"}, where the dataset is split into \textbf{training} and \textbf{validation} subsets using a deterministic hold-out strategy. This split is controlled by a fixed random seed to guarantee reproducibility. The training loader is configured with \textbf{shuffling}, \textbf{batching}, and optional worker parallelism to maximize throughput during optimization, while the validation loader preserves deterministic ordering to ensure stable evaluation. Additional constraints such as \textbf{maximum sample limits} can be applied to both subsets, which is particularly useful for debugging or resource-constrained environments. This stage effectively defines how data flows into the training loop.

The \textbf{fourth stage} is the \textbf{"U-Net Construction"}, which represents the core of the model architecture. The network is structured as a \textbf{symmetric encoder-decoder system} with \textbf{skip connections}.The encoder progressively reduces \textbf{spatial resolution} while increasing \textbf{feature dimensionality}, whereas the decoder reconstructs the spatial structure from the compressed representation. At the center of the architecture lies a \textbf{bottleneck block} that processes the most compressed representation of the data. Each level of the network is composed of \textbf{residual blocks}, which integrate convolutional feature extraction with \textbf{temporal conditioning} through sinusoidal time embeddings. Additionally, \textbf{attention blocks} are selectively applied at specific resolutions to capture \textbf{long-range spatial dependencies}. This architecture is specifically designed to balance \textbf{local detail preservation} with \textbf{global structural understanding}.

The \textbf{fifth stage} implements the \textbf{"Flow Match"}, which is the core part of the system. Here the network learns a \textbf{continuous velocity field} that defines how a sample evolves from \textbf{Gaussian noise} toward the data manifold. During training, \textbf{random noise} is sampled, a continuous time variable is drawn, and intermediate states along the interpolation trajectory are constructed. The model is then trained to predict the correct velocity at each point in this trajectory.

The \textbf{sixth stage} is the \textbf{"Training Loop"}, where optimization is performed over multiple epochs. In each iteration, batches of images are processed by the model, and the predicted velocity field is compared against the target \textbf{Flow Matching objective}. Gradients are computed through backpropagation and stabilized using \textbf{gradient clipping} to prevent exploding updates. The model was trained using optimizers such as \textbf{SGD and AdamW}.Validation is performed after each epoch to monitor generalization performance, while periodic logging provides insight into training dynamics.

The \textbf{final stage} is \textbf{"Checkpoint Saving and Sampling"}. At regular intervals, the entire training state is serialized, including \textbf{model weights}, \textbf{optimizer state}, and training metadata such as the current epoch and configuration arguments. This enables seamless resumption of training and full experiment traceability. In addition, the system periodically generates samples by solving the learned velocity field using a \textbf{numerical integration scheme}, producing synthetic images from pure Gaussian noise. These samples are saved as visual grids, providing a qualitative measure of model performance throughout training.


\section{U-Net Architecture}
This is the core of the model, and its role is to approximate the \textbf{velocity field} required by the Flow Matching objective. The entire architecture is built using a \textbf{modular "block-based" design}, where each component is implemented as an independent and reusable module, including \textbf{Residual Blocks, Attention Blocks, Downsampling Blocks and Upsampling Blocks}.

The network follows a \textbf{symmetric encoder-decoder structure with skip connections} for efficient feature extraction and spatial reconstruction.

The entire system is \textbf{configurable through CLI arguments}. In addition, the codebase was written using a \textbf{clean, modular, and highly commented structure} to improve \textbf{readability, maintainability, and extensibility}.
\noindent
\subsection{Residual Block}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{ResidualBlock_none.png}
\end{center}

\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

The Residual Block is the unit of the U-Net that perform \textbf{feature extraction}.  

It is composed of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1) Normalization} The "Group Normalization, was chosen instead of BatchNorm due to stability on variable batch sizes.
    \item \textbf{2) Two 3×3 Convolutions}, which provide local receptive field expansion while maintaining spatial resolution.
    \item \textbf{3) SiLU activation}, which was selected because it provided smoother gradients compared to ReLU (but both of them were tried).
    \item \textbf{4) Time Embedding injection}, where the time embedding is added to the feature map to condition the network on the timestep $\tau$.
    \item \textbf{5) Residual Skip Connection}, which either preserves identity or applies a 1×1 convolution if the channel dimensions differ.
\end{itemize}

The choice of a \textbf{residual structure} was made to prevent vanishing gradients, ensure stable training, and for potential reusability in future works.

\end{minipage}

\vspace{0.8em}
\hrule
\vspace{0.8em}

\noindent
\subsection{Attention Block}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{AttentionBlock_none.png}
\end{center}

\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

The Attention Block implements \textbf{self-attention over spatial feature maps}, allowing the model to capture \textbf{long-range dependencies} that convolutions cannot model efficiently.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1) Normalization}, Group Normalization is applied before attention to stabilize feature distributions.
    
    \item \textbf{2) QKV Convolution}, a $1 \times 1$ convolution is used to project features into Query, Key, and Value representations.
    
    \item \textbf{3) Scaling factor $C^{-0.25}$}, applied to the QK dot-product to control its magnitude and improve the numerical stability of the softmax.
    
    \item \textbf{4) Softmax}, applied over the scaled QK similarities to convert them into a probability distribution:
    \[
    \text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{softmax}\left(C^{-0.25} QK^\top \right)V
    \]
    
    \item \textbf{5) Residual connection}, Was used to ensure that attention refines features instead of replacing them entirely.
\end{itemize}

This block is applied only at selected resolutions (\textbf{"--AttentionResolutions"}) due to its high computational cost.

\end{minipage}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.45\linewidth]{UpsampleBlock_none.png}
\end{center}

\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}

The Upsampling Block reconstructs spatial resolution in the decoder path of the U-Net. It consists of:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{1) Upsampling}, of type "Nearest-neighbor", used to increase the spatial resolution of the feature maps.
    \item \textbf{2) 3×3 convolution}, used to refine the upsampled features and reduce artifacts.
\end{itemize}

This block allows the network to recover spatial details while combining coarse semantic information with high-resolution features through skip connections.

\subsection{Time Conditioning}

The model is conditioned on a continuous scalar variable \textbf{$\tau \in [0,1]$}. This variable represents the progression along the generative process, and the main role of $\tau$ is to \textbf{inform the network about the current stage of the transformation}.
This allows the U-Net to \textbf{adapt its prediction depending on the noise level}.

To encode this scalar into a format suitable for the U-Net, a \textbf{sinusoidal positional encoding} is used. This encoding maps $\tau$ into a higher-dimensional representation using sine and cosine functions at multiple frequencies. The main reason for using this encoding is that it provides a \textbf{smooth, continuous representation of time}, allowing the model to \textbf{generalize to unseen values of $\tau$} without requiring additional parameters.

The resulting time embedding is injected into the network inside each Residual Block. This conditioning mechanism ensures that every block in the network is explicitly aware of the current time step, enabling \textbf{consistent modulation of features across the entire U-Net hierarchy}.

\section{Training via Flow Matching}

The training procedure is formulated as a \textbf{regression problem over a continuous-time vector field}. 
The goal is not to directly model the data distribution, but to learn a function that predicts how a point evolves from a noise sample to a data sample along a continuous trajectory.

\vspace{0.5em}
\subsection{Time variable and sampling.}
A continuous time variable $\tau$ is first sampled from a uniform distribution $\mathcal{U}(0,1)$ and then rescaled using: $\tau = \varepsilon + (1 - 2\varepsilon)u$ into the restricted distribution:
\[
\mathcal{U}(\varepsilon, 1 - \varepsilon)
\]

This choice removes samples too close to the boundary values $\tau = 0$ and $\tau = 1$, which correspond to the extreme regions, excluding these improves numerical stability during training and provides a more informative learning signal.\\
In the implementation and in all reported experiments, $\varepsilon$ is fixed in the range $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$, as this setting provides a stable numerical regime while maintaining accurate coverage of the trajectory.

\vspace{0.9em}

\subsection{Construction of training trajectories.}
For each data sample $\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}$, a noise sample is drawn from:
$\mathbf{z}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.Then a point along the interpolation trajectory is constructed using the \textbf{Rectified Flow (linear interpolation) path}, defined as:
\[
\mathbf{z}_\tau = (1 - \tau)\mathbf{z}_0 + \tau \mathbf{x}.
\]

This formulation induces a deterministic straight-line trajectory in data space that continuously connects noise samples to data samples.
\subsection{Target velocity field.}
By differentiating the interpolation path with respect to $\tau$, we obtain the \textbf{conditional velocity field}, which serves as the supervision target for $v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_\tau, \tau)$:
\[
\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{z}_\tau \mid \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}_0)
= \frac{d\mathbf{z}_\tau}{d\tau}
= \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}_0.
\]

This velocity field is constant along each trajectory and corresponds to the ground-truth transport direction used for supervision during training.
\vspace{0.9em}

\subsection{Learning objective.} \\
The model is trained by minimizing a mean squared error between the predicted velocity field and the ground-truth target induced by the interpolation path:
\[
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) =
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}_0, \tau}
\left[
\left\|
v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_\tau, \tau) - (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{z}_0)
\right\|^2
\right].
\]

\vspace{0.9em}

\subsection{Optimization procedure.} \\
In early experiments, Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) was evaluated, but AdamW provided faster convergence and more stable training.

\[
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}.
\]

Training proceeds over multiple epochs, alternating between sampling $(\mathbf{z}_\tau, \tau)$ pairs, predicting the velocity field with the U-Net backbone, computing the Flow Matching loss, and performing backpropagation.
\vspace{0.9em}\section{Sampling procedure (ODE-based generation)}
Sampling is implemented as a deterministic pipeline executed inside the \texttt{GenerateSamples()} function. Given identical inputs (seed, checkpoint, and hyperparameters), the output is fully reproducible.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Initial condition (seeded noise generation)}

The sampling process starts from Gaussian noise:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
\end{equation}

In the implementation, this tensor is generated using a fixed random seed. This design choice ensures reproducibility across runs, which is essential for debugging, benchmarking, and model comparison. The seed effectively controls the entire generation process.



The continuous interval $t \in [0,1]$ is divided into $N$ uniform time steps:
\begin{equation}
\Delta t = \frac{1}{N}.
\end{equation}

This value controls the trade-off between speed and quality: a smaller number of steps reduces inference time, while a larger number of steps improves the accuracy of the generated trajectory at the cost of additional forward passes through the network.

\subsection{Model evaluation and correction}

At each sampling step, the U-Net is executed to compute a velocity field:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_1 = v_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t).
\end{equation}
Then a intermediate prediction  state is computed using an Euler update,this is not used as the final output, but serves as a candidate state to improve the approximation of the dynamics.
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}^* = \mathbf{z}_t + \Delta t \cdot \mathbf{v}_1.
\end{equation}

To correct the error introduced by the linear approximation, the network is evaluated again at the predicted state:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v}_2 = v_\theta(\mathbf{z}^*, t + \Delta t).
\end{equation}

Although it requires an additional forward pass per step, this design choice improves stability and reduces accumulated integration error without increasing model complexity.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Integration scheme (Heun solver)}

The final update combines both velocity estimates (v1 and v2):
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{t+\Delta t} =
\mathbf{z}_t + \frac{\Delta t}{2}(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2).
\end{equation}

This implementation corresponds to a Heun (RK2) solver. Compared to a pure Euler update, this choice reduces accumulated integration error and improves sample consistency, while still being simple to implement and stable in practice.

% ============================================================
\subsection{Output post-processing (data-space constraint)}

After the final iteration, the output is clamped to the training range:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_N \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}(\mathbf{z}_N, -1, 1).
\end{equation}

This step is required because the model is trained on normalized data in $[-1,1]$. Without this constraint, small numerical drifts during integration could produce invalid pixel ranges, leading to artifacts in visualization.

\section{Experimental Validation}

To evaluate the performance of the proposed Flow Matching model, experiments were conducted on four benchmark datasets with increasing complexity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MNIST}: grayscale handwritten digits.
    \item \textbf{Fashion-MNIST}: grayscale clothing items.
    \item \textbf{Animal Faces}: colored dataset with higher visual complexity and texture diversity.
\end{itemize}

The evaluation of the Flow Matching model is performed through three complementary aspects: (i) qualitative sample generation, (ii) analysis of the ODE-based generative process, and (iii) training stability across datasets of increasing complexity (MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and Animal Faces).

\section{The use of AI Agents in this process}

During the development of this project, AI agents were used as a supportive tool throughout different stages of the project. Their usage was limited to support, and did not replaced in any way my core decisions.\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Requirement Analysis.}
AI tools were used to retrieve official documentation and technical resources in order to better understand the theoretical foundations of the project and discover new techniques. 
A relevant example is the study of the \textit{Time Embedding} mechanism.

\item \textbf{Design and Implementation Support.}
AI assistance was used to improve code structure by identifying reusable components and reducing redundancy.
In particular, it helped in designing utility modules such as \textit{Identity Blocks}, which simplified and modularized the implementation.

\item \textbf{Kaggle Execution and Visualization.}
AI tools were used to generate and adapt code for running experiments on Kaggle, including data processing pipelines, model execution commands, and visualization scripts for plots and results.

\item \textbf{Organization and Checkpointing.}
AI assistance supported the correct implementation of checkpoint saving/loading and sampling procedures within the Kaggle environment.

\end{itemize}
Throughout the project, AI-generated suggestions were critically evaluated and frequently challenged through independent study and verification. I systematically consulted official documentation, academic papers, video lectures, lecture slides provided during the course, and additional handouts distributed by the professor. This process contributed to several design and training decisions, including the adoption of a progressive multi-resolution training strategy (32$\times$32 $\rightarrow$ 64$\times$64 $\rightarrow$ 96$\times$96). Overall, AI agents were used solely as supportive tools for learning, debugging, and to speedup some developement secondary options, while all final architectural decisions, experiments, and implementations remained fully under my control.
